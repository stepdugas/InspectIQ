# Termite Inspection Report Template: A Complete Guide for Home Inspectors

When you're conducting termite inspections day after day, the last thing you want to spend hours doing is writing detailed reports from scratch. A well-structured termite inspection report template saves time while ensuring you capture every critical finding—from visible damage to conducive conditions that need addressing. Whether you're a solo inspector or managing a team, having a proven template framework keeps your reports consistent, professional, and thorough. ## What Should Be Included in a Termite Inspection Report?

A comprehensive termite inspection report goes far beyond simply noting whether termites are present. Your report serves as a critical document for homeowners, real estate agents, and sometimes lenders—each with specific information needs. Here's what your template should cover:

**Property Details & Inspection Scope**
Start with the basics: property address, inspection date, inspector name, and client information. Clearly define the scope of your inspection. Are you inspecting the entire structure, or only accessible areas? Will you be performing destructive testing? Setting expectations upfront prevents misunderstandings later.

**Exterior Observations**
Document the foundation type, any visible cracks or gaps, wood-to-soil contact, mulch conditions, and landscaping issues. Look for mud tubes, which are a telltale sign of subterranean termites. Note the condition of exterior wood, siding, and any areas where water damage is present.

**Interior Findings**
Systematically document conditions in basements, crawl spaces, attics, and living areas. Include observations about wood damage severity, whether it's active or dormant, and locations mapped to the property layout.

**Pest History & Treatment Records**
Ask about previous termite treatments and request documentation. This context helps you interpret current conditions and explain why certain areas may show old versus new damage.

**Recommendations**
Your professional recommendations are perhaps the most valuable part of your report. Whether you're suggesting professional treatment, monitoring, or preventative measures, your expert judgment guides the homeowner's next steps.

## Key Sections for Your Template Structure

Building a reusable template starts with organizing information logically. Most inspectors find success with this structure:

**Summary Section**
Begin with an executive summary stating your overall findings. A homeowner should understand the termite situation within the first few sentences. Is there active termite damage? Are conducive conditions present? What's the urgency level?

**Detailed Observations Section**
Organize by location: Foundation, Crawl Space, Basement, First Floor, Second Floor, Attic, etc. For each area, note what you observed, what conditions you found, and what (if any) evidence of termite activity existed. Be specific about measurements when documenting damage extent.

**Active vs. Dormant Damage**
Your template should differentiate between active infestations and past damage. Active damage might show sawdust frass, hollow-sounding wood, or visible termites. Dormant or historical damage shows the previous extent of the problem but indicates no current activity. This distinction matters enormously to the homeowner.

**Conducive Conditions**
Many homeowners don't realize that even without active termites, their property may invite infestation. Your template should have a dedicated section for conducive conditions: wood mulch against the foundation, poor drainage, excessive moisture in crawl spaces, cracks in the foundation, or wood-to-soil contact. This forward-thinking observation adds tremendous value.

**Photos and Diagrams**
Your template should include space for annotated photos. Visual evidence is far more persuasive than text alone. Consider including a simple floor plan or diagram where you can mark inspection areas and note findings by location.

## How to Customize a Template for Different Property Types

Not all properties are created equal, and your template should account for structural variations. A crawl space inspection differs significantly from inspecting a slab-on-grade foundation or a post-and-pier structure common in coastal areas.

**Single-Family Homes**
Most standard templates work well here. You'll have access to crawl spaces, basements, or both, plus attic space. Your template sections should flow with the typical inspection path: exterior, foundation, crawl space, interior, attic.

**Condominiums and Multi-Unit Properties**
These require noting shared walls and limited access. Your template might need flexibility to document "not inspected" areas with clear explanations. Is there a shared crawl space? Can you access neighboring units? Be transparent about limitations.

**Commercial Properties**
Commercial structures often have larger floor plans, multiple roof sections, and extensive wall cavities. Your template might benefit from section-by-section documentation with numbered areas for clarity.

**Older Historic Homes**
Historic properties often have unique structural materials—old-growth timber, adobe, or stone foundations. Your template should accommodate notes about material type and condition, as termite damage manifests differently in various materials.

## Best Practices for Termite Report Documentation

Beyond having a solid template, certain practices elevate the professionalism and usefulness of your reports. First, use consistent terminology. When describing damage severity, consider using a scale: "minor surface damage," "moderate damage affecting structural integrity," or "severe damage requiring immediate treatment." This gives readers context beyond just "termite damage present."

Second, photograph everything, but photograph strategically. Include wide shots showing context, close-ups showing specific damage, and photos showing the absence of damage in thoroughly inspected areas. This protects you professionally and gives the homeowner confidence in your thoroughness.

Third, document your inspection methodology. Did you use a moisture meter? Did you probe wood with a screwdriver? Your template should note the tools and techniques you employed, demonstrating the rigor of your work. Finally, always include clear next steps. Don't leave a homeowner uncertain about what to do with your findings. Whether it's "consult a licensed pest control professional" or "implement these moisture management recommendations," your guidance should be actionable.

## Frequency and Follow-Up: When to Update Your Template

Your template isn't set-it-and-forget-it. As you gain experience and encounter different scenarios, your template should evolve. Every few years, review your past reports. Are there sections you always expand? Are there questions that homeowners frequently ask? These are signals to adjust your template.

Also stay informed about regional termite species and local conditions. If your area experiences a shift in termite populations or if new construction methods become common, your template should reflect this knowledge. Engaging with local pest control professionals and attending inspector education courses keeps your template relevant and comprehensive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What's the difference between a termite inspection and a general home inspection?

A termite inspection focuses specifically on evidence of termite activity and conditions that invite termites. While general home inspectors note pest issues as they observe them, a dedicated termite inspection is more thorough and specialized. Some jurisdictions require separate credentials for termite inspections, so understand your local licensing requirements.

### How detailed should my termite report template be?

Your report should be detailed enough that a third party reading it understands exactly what you found and why it matters. However, it shouldn't be so verbose that homeowners become overwhelmed. Aim for clarity and completeness, using your template sections to organize information logically. Photos should supplement your written observations, not replace them.

### Should I recommend specific pest control treatments in my report?

No. As a home inspector, your role is to identify and document conditions. Treatment recommendations should come from licensed pest control professionals. Your job is to say, "Evidence of termite activity is present" or "Conducive conditions exist," and refer the homeowner to appropriate professionals. This protects your liability and respects the specialized expertise of pest control operators.

### Can I use the same template for all property types?

While a base template can serve multiple property types, you should customize it for significant structural differences. A crawl space inspection section won't apply to a slab-on-grade home. Your template should be flexible enough to accommodate variations while maintaining consistency in how you document findings.

### How often should I update my template?

Review and update your template annually, or whenever you encounter a scenario your current template doesn't handle well. Stay current with industry standards, state regulations (even if those regulations don't dictate report content), and best practices shared within the home inspection community. Your template should evolve as your expertise deepens.
